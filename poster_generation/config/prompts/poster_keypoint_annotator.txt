The goal is to identify the key points that SHOULD appear on a good academic poster derived from this paper, specify the best reading order of those key points on the poster, and label each key point with the CORRESPONDING TOP-LEVEL (FIRST-LEVEL) SECTION ONLY from the original academic paper.

A poster is highly space-limited. Therefore, you must prioritize only the most important, poster-worthy content. Your output will be used to evaluate whether an agent-generated poster contains the right content and presents it in a sensible reading order.

STRICT POSTER MINDSET:
You must imagine that the poster has very limited space and attention. A good poster does NOT include everything from the paper. A good poster highlights only the essential storyline:
- What problem is being solved
- Why it matters
- What is the core idea / method
- What is novel
- What setup or evidence is necessary to understand the contribution
- What are the most important results
- What is the key takeaway / conclusion / impact

You must think like a human making difficult trade-offs:
- If something is correct but low-priority, exclude it.
- If multiple details are similar, merge them into one higher-level key point.
- If many experiments are reported, keep only the most poster-worthy findings.
- If the paper includes long background or literature review, compress or omit it unless it is absolutely necessary for understanding the contribution.

SELECTION PRINCIPLES:
A good poster key point usually belongs to one of the following categories:
- Problem / task definition
- Core motivation or gap
- Main contribution
- Central method idea / mechanism / pipeline
- Crucial model or system component
- Experimental setup only if necessary for interpreting results
- Most important empirical result(s)
- Most decision-relevant comparison or advantage
- Main conclusion / takeaway / implication

A key point should be included if it satisfies one or more of these:
- It is essential for understanding the paper's main contribution
- It is likely to be placed prominently on a poster
- It represents a headline claim or result
- It helps a reader reconstruct the poster's main narrative quickly
- Removing it would noticeably reduce the quality of poster content

A key point should be excluded if it is mainly:
- background filler
- related work survey
- secondary or tertiary result
- low-level procedural detail
- repetitive elaboration
- detail that belongs in a paper but not on a poster

RESULT-SELECTION RULES:
Experimental results and analysis must be heavily filtered.
A poster usually shows only the most important evidence.
Therefore:
- Include only the most central result(s), not the full experimental inventory.
- Prefer headline performance, strongest comparison, most convincing finding, or one especially informative ablation/analysis.
- Exclude minor or repetitive results.
- Exclude fine-grained metric lists unless the exact metric is core to the claim.
- Exclude secondary analyses unless they materially strengthen understanding of the main contribution.

1. problem / motivation

3. method / mechanism
4. strongest evidence / results
5. takeaway / implication

However, do NOT follow this template blindly. Use the actual paper content and choose the most natural poster reading flow for this specific paper. The reading order should feel like a coherent visual narrative for a poster viewer.
